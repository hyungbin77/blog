Social anxiety can be overwhelming especially because of its paradoxical nature. It is completely invisible and at the same time it isolates you and prevents you from reaching out for help without feeling ashamed or humiliated. You keep quiet and hope against your heart of hearts that someone will notice that you’re not speaking, that you may be uncomfortable, that you feel as if you don’t belong. But it’s very rare for people to pick up on it just like that - and that’s usually because we’re so good at hiding it. Sometimes, our invisibility protects us, like camouflage. Sometimes it gives us the illusion of control.
I speak from personal experience. All my life growing up I was bullied, and I went through many different schools in different countries, each time a different kind of hazing, and new brand of cruelty. So by the time I got to college, I was a silent bundle of nerves. Even so, I was slowly able to open myself up once I gave myself the freedom to push the imaginary “reset” button on my social life. I decided to dive deep into my interests and let that guide me to “my people.” There were a lot of misses, but I kept pushing myself to go to different clubs because honestly, I had nothing to lose. Inevitably at parties or completely unstructured social settings I would lapse into silence and grow even more lonely than how I felt when I was actually alone. But then there were a few places where I met people who were also awkward like me, but more importantly, they didn’t take themselves too seriously, and suddenly I started to feel like I belonged. So I kept going back. For me, it was clubs like the “Tree House Society” that allowed me to relive my childhood in healing way, and salsa dancing. The one passion that changed my life and helped me find my closest friends, though, was poetry. I found a weekly open mic and finally felt like I was part of something real & meaningful, where the people I met and surrounded myself with were kind and open by their very nature.
Still, it took me years to overcome my social anxiety. Indeed, it’s still a part of me, and sometimes I still feel a bit awkward or shy in crowded/noisy places with strangers. But I am much more aware of this feeling now, and I know how to push past it and also when to “pick my battles,” so to speak. Every once in a while when you get bad vibes when you’re around a certain person, you may realize that it’s not just you. We tend to be very sensitive and pick up on tiny social cues that most people don’t see. The problem is, we become overly sensitive to all the negative social cues and focus only on those, freaking out over how much this person doesn’t like you or is sick of talking to you, to the point that you completely ignore any good vibes they’re trying to send your way!
I think that firstly, as many others have stated, you should try to accept yourself for who you are, and try to find the strengths in the qualities that you regard as weaknesses. Our sensitivity, for example, may be painful, but it also means we can get to the heart of things and can empathize on a more immediate and deep level with almost anybody. Our “obssessiveness” that manifests as constant worries can be redirected into thoughtfulness and carefulness. Sometimes it’s just a matter of redirecting that nervous energy. Other times - if you find yourself feeling insecure on a deeper level, like if deep down you are afraid you might be “broken” somehow - it’s good to express these thoughts, fears, and worries to someone you trust. Don’t trust anyone like that just yet? That’s ok - take it as an opportunity to learn to trust in yourself. Keep a journal or diary if you don’t already. Self-awareness - in a compassionate way, not in a critical way - is key to overcoming social anxiety. Therapy is wonderful because it gives you safe space to explore all your doubts and gives you a chance to dispel some of the pessimistic assumptions you might have developed. I highly recommend it. My own therapy was instrumental in getting me to where I am now, and I’m still in treatment because there is still so much I have to learn about myself and the way I interact with the world.
The hardest lesson I had to learn was that, 99% of the time, it’s not about me. Like, literally, I would believe that people were thinking all these bad thoughts about me, when in reality, I wasn’t in their thoughts at all. And that’s the thing: people talk about themselves and think about themselves soooo much more than they let on. So I can guarantee you that when people lose interest in talking to you or seem aloof or whatever, it’s almost always about something going on in their head and nothing at all whatsoever to do with you. This realization, once you actually see it, is freeing, in a way. Because even though it sucks when people are completely indifferent to you, that is infinitely better than the fantasy that you are broken and everyone avoids you because there’s something wrong with you. Trust me: it’s a fantasy, nothing more. It’s the ones who DON’T worry about whether they are a good person or not, who DON’T care how harmful their actions are, that are more broken and rigid than you will ever be. And, unfortunately, that’s quite common as well. But you can learn one thing from them. At the end of the day, it REALLY DOESN’T MATTER WHAT PEOPLE THINK OF YOU. In fact, if there are people who actually dislike you or hate you, that’s a good sign that you’re doing something right. Authenticity will have the magical effect of filtering out all the people who keep you from being yourself and will lead you to the people who accept you as you are. You just have to stick to it and be patient.
So, in short, learn to accept yourself by finding strengths in your weaknesses, remember that most of the time people are NOT thinking of you, and give yourself the freedom and dignity of being authentic no matter how anyone judges you - because it’s the ones who judge others who are actually broken and keep us locked in the vicious cycle of self-loathing that is social anxiety. Trust in yourself first and foremost, and your authentic self will open doors you never knew existed. Trust in your capacity for love."

I used to be very socially anxious during high school and my early university years. If I had visited a Psychologist at the time I am sure I would've met criteria for a Social Phobia diagnosis. I used to do things like avoid eye contact, say only nice, boring stuff to make sure I didn't get people offside, if I had a difficult conversation with someone I'd then have a mental map of their predicted movements and avoid them like the plague, and if I still ran into them I'd often pretend I had not seen them. If someone was overly confident, rude or abrupt, I'd do anything I could to please them so they wouldn't challenge me or single me out, even if it meant behaving contrary to my personal values and beliefs. If I was talking to a conservative person I'd profess only conservative views, if I was around a liberal or left wing person I'd express only liberal or left wing views. I basically had no stable sense of my own identity, and what fragments of identity I had developed were buried deep inside for nobody to see. I had residual depressed mood and probably also met criteria for Generalised Anxiety Disorder, so the worrier part of me was very aware that I was wasting my life and relationships, and it worried constantly about my future. It told me I would never have a good friend who liked me for who I was, I would never have a job I enjoyed because I wasn't confident enough to pursue anything interesting, and I would never marry or have children because I was too shy to tell someone I had feelings for them. I ate my feelings away for years, so I was close to the obese weight range throughout most of high school, which didn't help my confidence or mood at all. I stayed at home either studying, binge watching TV shows or with my nose buried deep in a book. That was the way I explored the world at that time, and that was where I got my pleasure from.
The first time I realised there was a name for the unhelpful feelings and behaviour patterns I had experienced for so many years was during my third year Psychopathology subject at university. At that time I realised that what I experienced was not normal, but I wasn't the only person going through it either. I also learned that exposure is the key to overcoming it. Only then would I learn to engage with the world in meaningful ways, even when I felt anxious.
I gradually learned to make choices about what situations to push myself into and when to be kind to myself and give myself a break. I learned that taking risks is necessary to every meaningful relationship. Risking rejection by asking someone to hang out with you, risking offending someone by expressing your honest opinion, risking being judged by putting your true self out there for people to meet and risking copping blame by being decisive.
By taking risks and accepting vulnerabity, I now have a good group of true friends and a wonderful partner with whom I can be very honest, I have my dream job where I get to help people every day and be intellectually stimulated, and I can disagree with people and express my opinions in conversations with new people. I still feel somewhat panicked in many of these situations, and I still have the voice in my head telling me I don't have the right to be myself and nobody will like me if I am, but I’m learning more and more about when to ignore the voice, and the odd occasion where listening to it might still be a good idea."
